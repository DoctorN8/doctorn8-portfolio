AI Decision Coach

Practice responsible AI judgment before it becomes a workplace risk.

A functional, scenario-based learning prototype that helps operational teams decide when and how to use generative AI, recognize data and reliability risks, apply safeguards, and revise their reasoning with transparent feedback.

The challenge

Responsible AI use requires judgment, not just prompt skill.

In a risk-sensitive enterprise, operational teams may understand basic prompting yet still need to decide what data can be used, whether a tool is approved, how output should be verified, and when to involve a human reviewer or internal owner. This prototype turns that judgment into brief, observable practice.

The learning need is not introductory AI literacy. It is judgment under constraints: making a defensible choice despite time pressure, incomplete information, sensitive data, and varying levels of risk.

What I built

One customer-complaint-summary scenario with pathway selection, rationale and confidence capture, safeguard choices, policy-mapped rubric feedback, decision revision, transfer practice, and browser-local event capture through localStorage.

Design choices

Three deliberate design decisions

Decision before coaching

The learner commits to a pathway and explains the reasoning before guidance appears. This preserves judgment practice, avoids turning the coach into an answer generator, and creates a baseline for revision.

Diagnostic feedback

Privacy and data handling, tool authorization, reliability and verification, fairness and bias, and human accountability remain distinct. This makes feedback actionable and supports targeted improvement rather than a generic correct/incorrect response.

Bounded automation

Deterministic rubric logic is mapped to fictional policy guidance. No generative model is treated as the authority for learner evaluation, and escalation remains a valid response when available guidance does not resolve the situation.

Traceable trade-offs

Design-decision ledger

The prototype makes key trade-offs explicit so that learning, UX, and AI-governance choices can be reviewed and refined with stakeholders.

Decision Alternative considered Why this version
Learner decides before feedback Reveal the policy answer before the learner acts Preserves active judgment practice and creates a baseline for feedback and revision.
Separate risk dimensions Give a single overall safe/unsafe score Helps learners identify the type of reasoning gap: data handling, authorization, reliability, fairness, or accountability.
Fictional policy corpus Open-web or unrestricted retrieval Keeps the prototype controlled, traceable, safe for demonstration, and free of external-policy ambiguity.
Deterministic rubric feedback LLM-only scoring Avoids making a generative model the sole authority for a high-stakes learning judgment.
Escalation is a valid pathway Force a use-AI or do-not-use-AI binary Models responsible uncertainty and organizational accountability.
One complete scenario loop Build a broad content library before testing the pattern Prioritizes a testable end-to-end learning experience over superficial feature breadth.
Browser-local event capture Claim enterprise telemetry before implementing it Demonstrates an observable prototype event model without overstating xAPI, LMS, LRS, or production analytics capability.

Inclusive interaction

Accessibility in the prototype

The prototype includes accessible web interaction patterns. The items below describe implemented features; formal accessibility validation remains proposed work.

Implemented patterns

  • Semantic HTML structure, landmarks, headings, labeled form controls, and fieldsets
  • Keyboard-operable buttons, decision cards, form inputs, and links
  • Visible keyboard focus states
  • Clear validation and error messaging for required learner inputs
  • Text labels that do not rely on color alone
  • Plain-language instructions and feedback
  • Descriptive alternative text for meaningful screenshots

Planned validation

Before any production use, conduct a keyboard-only walkthrough, automated accessibility scan, contrast and zoom review, screen-reader-informed review, and usability testing with people who use assistive technologies.

Fictional policy guidance and how feedback is generated

The fictional Northstar Enterprise AI Use Standard provides the traceable policy basis for prototype feedback.

  • Data Classification and Approved Tools
  • Human Review and Accountability
  • Reliability and Source Verification
  • Fairness, Bias, and Escalation
  • Accessibility and Inclusive Communication

The deterministic rubric checks whether the learner’s selected pathway and safeguard choices address scenario-specific requirements. Feedback maps those requirements to the relevant fictional policy category.

The prototype is a learning aid. It does not make operational, legal, security, privacy, HR, or compliance decisions.

Proposed research and validation plan

Before production development, I would conduct 5–7 discovery interviews with representative operational staff, privacy and security partners, and managers. Findings would inform scenario realism, vocabulary, available decision paths, escalation ownership, and practical constraints.

A subsequent evaluation would assess decision reasoning, risk-signal recognition, confidence calibration, usability, and keyboard- and screen-reader-informed accessibility.

Evaluation question Proposed method Proposed measure
Can learners select an appropriate AI-use pathway? Scenario assessment plus expert-scored rubric Decision accuracy and rationale quality
Do learners recognize critical risk signals? Compare initial attempt with a transfer scenario Correctly identified privacy, reliability, fairness, and escalation signals
Does feedback change reasoning? Compare initial and revised rationales using the same rubric Change in decision quality
Are learners appropriately calibrated? Pair confidence ratings with rubric scores Reduction in confidence-performance mismatch
Is the experience usable? Moderated usability testing with representative learners Task success, completion time, errors, navigation friction, SUS, and qualitative feedback
Does the prototype create accessibility barriers? Keyboard-only test, contrast/type review, screen-reader-informed review, and assistive-technology user testing Completion and comprehension across interaction modes

Client collaboration approach

How I would use this in a client co-design session

This prototype is designed to support collaborative concept validation, not simply a finished demonstration. In a live client session, I would use the scenario and wireframes to align on the learner decision, risk taxonomy, policy boundaries, and measurement priorities before expanding development.

  1. Confirm the target audience, business context, and observable behavior change.
  2. Identify the highest-risk learner decision and the consequences of an incorrect choice.
  3. Review the learner pathway, feedback categories, and escalation ownership.
  4. Co-design one representative scenario and define its policy and rubric logic.
  5. Agree on prototype revisions, subject-matter-expert review, and pilot measures.
Reusable pattern: The value is not the specific customer-support scenario. It is the decision → safeguard → feedback → revision loop, which can be adapted only after validating the context, policy rules, and stakeholder ownership for a new use case.

Scope and next steps

What I would validate next

This MVP intentionally prioritizes one complete feedback-and-revision loop over feature breadth. It demonstrates a learning pattern and technical safety approach that could later be expanded through additional scenarios, organization-specific policy retrieval, role-based pathways, expert-review workflows, production analytics, LMS/LXP integration, and formal usability and learning-effectiveness studies.

  • Test whether learners interpret the four decision pathways as meaningfully distinct and know when escalation is appropriate.
  • Compare constrained-coach feedback with reviews from privacy, security, legal, learning, and domain subject-matter experts.
  • Conduct accessibility testing using keyboard-only navigation, zoom and contrast review, screen-reader-informed review, and sessions with users of assistive technologies.
  • Pilot the flow with representative operational staff and examine rationale quality, decision revisions, confidence calibration, transfer performance, and interaction friction.
  • Refine the rubric and policy-guidance logic before any use beyond a controlled learning prototype.
